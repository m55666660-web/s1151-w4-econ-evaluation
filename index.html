Economics of Education | Week 4: Economic Evaluation

Study Guide: How Do You Decide Whether an Education Investment Is Worth It?

Required: Levin Ch.8, Ch.10　·　Further reading: Levin Ch.1; McMahon (2009) Ch.3, Ch.4, Ch.5

1. Why these readings

For the past few weeks we have been asking whether education counts as an investment at all. This week the question gets more practical: if it is an investment, how do you work out whether it pays off? Economics of education gives you two tools for this, and it helps to think of them the way you would think about buying something:

Ch.8

Cost-effectiveness analysis (CE): comparison shopping

Given several ways to reach the same goal, which one is cheapest? The outcome is never converted into money, so CE can only tell you which option is the better buy — not whether the goal itself is worth pursuing.

Ch.10

Benefit-cost analysis (BC): should this business exist at all?

The benefits are also converted into money and discounted, so they can be added to or subtracted from costs. This can answer "is it worth it" — but only once you can put a dollar value on the benefit.

The instructor assigned Ch.8 and Ch.10 because you need both ends of this toolkit in hand. The other four readings are supporting cast: Ch.1 is the map, and the three McMahon chapters are real-world cases that show what these tools look like once you point them at higher education, including where they get tricky.

3. Paper-by-paper guide: why read it, and how

Ch.8Requiredpp.165–192

Levin Ch.8 — Cost-Effectiveness Analysis

What it answers: Several alternatives aim at the same goal — which one costs the least?

Why read it: The CE ratio is the most common "comparison-shopping tool" in education policy. Whenever you later see "it costs this much to raise scores by one standard deviation" in a paper or a policy brief, this is the logic behind it.

How to read it:

  • Start with 8.1 and Table 8.1 (pp.166–168) and hold onto "the CE ratio is the price of one unit of outcome."
  • 8.3 and 8.4 are the heart of the chapter (pp.173–183): interpretation, the sign trap, the CE plane, incremental analysis (ICER). Work through Table 8.4 by hand.
  • The evidence in 8.5 (Table 8.6, Example 8.2, Table 8.7, Table 8.8) does not need a close read — take away the author's conclusions: don't rank across contexts, "most effective" is not the same as "most cost-effective," and averages can hide heterogeneity.
  • End-of-chapter exercise 3 (p.192) is worth doing: it's about ICER and dominance.

Five things to keep in view:

  • The CE ratio is an efficiency metric, not a required budget, and it does not guarantee an outcome (p.176).
  • CE analysis cannot tell you whether something is "worth it" — that needs a willingness-to-pay figure or a BC analysis (pp.175, 179, 181).
  • Check the sign first: a ratio of −$500 can be the best option or the worst (Table 8.3).
  • Omitting induced costs can badly understate the true ratio (pp.182–183).
  • An average CE ratio can hide heterogeneity across groups (pp.187–189).

Note: The chapter assumes costs and effects have already been precisely estimated; uncertainty is deferred to Ch.11 (p.166). Don't worry yet about "how precise is precise" — focus on learning how to compare.

Ch.10Requiredpp.221–243

Levin Ch.10 — Benefit-Cost Analysis

What it answers: Does this investment's own benefits outweigh its own costs?

Why read it: To convince a finance office, a legislator, or a school board that "this is worth funding," you eventually have to run a BC analysis. This chapter covers NPV, the benefit-cost ratio, and IRR — and every trap that comes with each.

How to read it:

  • 10.1 (pp.222–229) walks through every indicator using one "adult literacy program" example. Get out a calculator and redo the arithmetic yourself (or use the calculator on the "Teaching Deck" tab): present value of benefits $557, NPV $257, benefit-cost ratio 1.86, IRR 34.9%, break-even in year three. Doing the arithmetic beats reading it ten times.
  • 10.2 (pp.229–232) sets the formulas aside and talks about how not to be fooled: perspective, distribution, differences in study design, statistical significance.
  • The cases in 10.3 (pp.233–239): start with the three-account tables for Perry (Example 10.1) and Chicago CPC (Table 10.2); then the youth program (NGYCP); then make sure you read 10.3.4 on welfare-to-work programs, which shows that education investments do not always have a positive NPV.
  • Example 10.2 (pp.234–236) covers "the return to an extra year of schooling," which connects directly to McMahon Ch.3 — read the five critiques while you're there.

Five things to keep in view:

  • NPV is driven by scale, the benefit-cost ratio's "per dollar" framing is a simplification, and IRR is very sensitive to changes in the cash flow.
  • The three indicators are complements; the book recommends reporting all three (p.229).
  • State the perspective clearly: society, the taxpayer, and the participant give different answers (pp.230–231).
  • Statistical significance is not the bar for including a benefit; what BC actually needs to test is "is NPV greater than zero" (p.232).
  • Most, but not all, education investments have a positive NPV (pp.239–240).
Ch.1Further reading · pp.1–28

Levin Ch.1 — Introduction

Read it as a map, not closely. Just look at three things: Table 1.1 (comparing the analysis types), p.21 (the one-line distinction between CE and BC), and p.24 (productive vs. allocative efficiency).

Angle: CE asks "which alternative reaches X at the lowest cost"; BC asks "should we invest in alternative Y, which produces X" (p.21). Table 1.1 lists five types — cost analysis, cost-feasibility, CE, CU, and BC — and all of them cost things out with the same "ingredients method" (p.24).

McMahon Ch.3Further reading · pp.69–117

McMahon Ch.3 — Higher Education and Economic Growth

A real, IRR-flavored BC analysis: costs (institutional costs plus forgone earnings) and benefits (the extra earnings after graduation), discounted, to get a rate of return compared against a 10% long-run benchmark (pp.93–98).

How to read it: catch four things — the skill deficit (real earnings for four-year college graduates rose about 48% from 1980 to 2007, pp.72–76); the true investment cost includes forgone earnings (pp.84–85); the definitions of social vs. private rates of return (pp.93–94); and the several critiques of "overeducation" and the author's rebuttals (pp.109–115).

McMahon Ch.4Further reading · pp.118–180

McMahon Ch.4 — Private Non-Market Benefits

Demonstrates how "turning a benefit into money" is actually done: benefits like health, children's outcomes, and longevity, converted from regression coefficients into a dollar figure. The private non-market benefit of a bachelor's degree comes to roughly $38,080 (2007 dollars), about 122% of the earnings gain (p.173).

How to read it: read the method, not the numbers — why the regression must control for income to avoid double counting (pp.122–123); the logic of the "income equivalent" conversion (p.160); and the author's own admission that the figures are imprecise and conservative (pp.162, 173).

McMahon Ch.5Further reading · pp.181–255

McMahon Ch.5 — Social Benefits and Policy Implications

The chapter that connects most directly to NPV, BCR, and IRR: p.188 states outright that the author uses IRR rather than NPV, and gives an example where NPV at 4% and at 6% discount differ enormously — a direct parallel to Ch.10's discount-rate and sensitivity discussion. Table 5.1 shows social and private rates of return side by side.

How to read it: catch three things — the three methods of estimating social benefits (pp.182–183); the IRR-vs-NPV argument (p.188); and the new social rates of return (pp.248–252). The empirical sections in between only need their headings and conclusions.

Lecture 1

The CE Ratio Is the Price of One Unit of Outcome

CE analysis is a comparative exercise: it evaluates alternative policies to find out which one reaches a shared goal at the lowest cost (p.165).

CE ratio = ΔCost ÷ ΔEffect = (CT − CC) ÷ (ET − EC)Decision rule: choose the alternative with the lowest cost per unit of effectiveness. "The ratio is the price of an outcome" (p.167).

1. Cost must match the effect

It has to cover all the resources that generate that effect, including induced costs.

2. Both sides are increments

Measured relative to business as usual — how much more spent, how much more gained.

3. The effect must be univariate

A single measure, identified with a valid method; how to choose one is covered in Ch.7.

4. Units must match

Cost and effect use the same unit — for instance, both "per student."

The CE Ratio Is an Efficiency Metric, Not a Budget

Talent Search: a multi-year program that helps low-income students finish high school and go on to college (Table 8.2, pp.173–176).

7,146participants
$3,900PV cost per person

768new college enrollees

$47,260

Cost per additional high school graduate (per additional college enrollee: $36,310). For comparison: a four-year public high school education costs about $45,000 per student.

How to read this number

  • It does not mean "you need to spend an extra $47,260," nor does it guarantee "one more graduate."
  • The book's own example: a program at $10,000 per unit is only twice as efficient as one at $20,000 per unit (p.176).
  • Whether it's worth it needs a willingness-to-pay figure or a BC analysis (pp.175, 179).
6
Don't mix the two Talent Search datasets

Table 8.2 (Bowden data): +8.2 percentage points, $3,900 per person. Example 8.2 (Hollands data): +10.8 percentage points, $3,590 per person.

Four relatives of CE (§8.2, pp.169–173)

　C ÷ utility score

Multiple outcomes combined into a single "utility" score, then divided by cost. Example: three special-education placements, CU ratios of $799 / $375 / $374.

　E ÷ C

How much effect a fixed sum, say $100,000, buys. Conclusions are the same as CE; the author recommends reporting the CE ratio consistently.

Expected-value CE　C ÷ E(effect)

Used when outcomes are uncertain. Example: dropout prevention, Program A $2,106 vs. Program B (expected 71.8 students) $1,394.

Hybrid CE ratio　(C − B) ÷ E

The numerator subtracts benefits already converted into money. Valid but rarely used: which benefits get subtracted feels arbitrary, and the numerator is no longer the program's cost.

Lecture 3

Check the Sign Before You Trust the Ratio: the CE Plane and ICER

ΔCostΔEffectCE ratioIn plain terms
A−$1,000+2−$500saves money and improves things (best case)
B+$1,000−2−$500costs money and makes things worse (worst case)
C+$1,000+2+$500costs more, but works better (a real trade-off)
D−$1,000−2+$500cheaper, but worse

A and B have identical ratios but opposite meanings; so do C and D. The ratio is undefined when the effect is zero, and it is discontinuous and asymmetric around zero — so always check the sign first (Table 8.3, pp.176–177).

9

The CE plane: placing alternatives into four quadrants

  • The willingness-to-pay line: for example, willingness to pay $100,000 per additional high school graduate (the book's own assumption). One side of the line is acceptable, the other isn't; CE analysis by itself cannot draw this line.
  • Talent Search's nine sites: eight fall in quadrant C, one in quadrant B (positive cost, negative effect). (Fig 8.2, p.178; the figure's title says "Eight Sites," the text and data say nine.)
10

ICER: climbing the ladder one step at a time (Table 8.4, pp.179–181)

  1. Sort alternatives by cost, low to high.
  2. Drop anything "costlier and less effective than another option" (strictly dominated): S5, S3, S2, S4.
  3. Compare each remaining alternative to the one before it: ICER = ΔCost ÷ ΔEffect.
  4. If the ICER falls instead of rising, the earlier one is weakly dominated — drop it and recompute.

Lecture 6

Each Indicator Has Its Own Trap — and You Always Have to Ask Whose Perspective

NPV is hostage to scale

A $20M program will almost always beat a $2M program even if its benefit is small. Switching to per-person NPV hides total resource needs and can wrongly suggest NPV is scale-invariant (p.224).

The benefit-cost ratio's "per dollar" is a simplification

It doesn't guarantee every additional dollar earns the same return. In the college-enrollment example, treating the induced $1M as a negative benefit gives 1.5, treating it as an induced cost gives 1.33 (p.225).

IRR is very sensitive to changes

Cost +10% ($330), benefit −10% ($135), and IRR drops from 34.9% to 23.1%. It still has to be compared against some benchmark rate (p.227).

Break-even (payback period): easy to communicate, but it makes a slow-payoff early investment look worse than it is; if the benefits fall on different groups, society may break even while individual groups never recoup their share (pp.228–229).

22

Three cautions for reading BC studies (pp.231–232, 227–228)

1. State the perspective

The default is "the whole of society," but it can be split into participants, taxpayers, and so on. Best practice is to report both the societal and the funder's perspective.

2. Statistical significance is not the entry ticket

BC analysis is meant to guide decisions, not to test hypotheses. Every estimated effect goes into the model with its standard error; the real test is "is NPV greater than zero."

3. ≠ IRR

Social return on investment often looks only at a single funder's own contribution, which is not a full societal perspective — so it is not IRR.

Rate-of-return studies: is another year of schooling worth it? (Example 10.2, pp.234–236)

The method

  • Use each education level's "age-earnings profile"
  • Benefit = the extra earnings the more-educated group gets at every age
  • Cost = forgone earnings (the opportunity cost) plus direct costs such as tuition
  • IRR = the discount rate that equates the present value of benefits and costs

Most studies find the IRR to a college degree is slightly lower on average than to primary schooling — a simple inference is that scarce funds are better spent on primary education.

The book's five critiques

  1. Assumes education's only benefit is earnings
  2. Measures only the quantity, not the quality, of education
  3. Assumes the only cost is forgone earnings, ignoring direct costs, which can be large
  4. Uses a single cross-section, assuming today's 65-year-old's earnings approximate what today's 25-year-old will earn 40 years from now
  5. Uses today's high school graduates as the counterfactual for what college graduates would have earned without a degree

This week's takeaway 1

The CE ratio is an "efficiency" figure — not a budget, and certainly not "is it worth it."

Four things to check with any number: is the cost complete, is it an increment or an average, whose perspective, and which group does it apply to.

BC analysis can say no: not every education investment has a positive NPV.
